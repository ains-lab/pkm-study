## Abstract

With the increasing frequency of APT attacks, cyber defense urgently demands high-quality threat intelligence support. Cyber threat intelligence (CTI) knowledge graphs have demonstrated significant potential in aiding threat detection and behavioral reasoning. However, existing CTI data often suffer from unstructured formats, fragmented knowledge, a reliance on manual annotation, and limited semantic mapping to attack techniques. These limitations hinder the robustness and accuracy of downstream reasoning tasks (e.g., attack attribution and intent inference). Moreover, traditional information extraction methods struggle to generalize in scenarios involving cross-paragraph dependencies, emerging threats, and low-resource samples, exhibiting weaknesses in context awareness and sensitivity to prompt variations. To this end, we propose CTI-Thinker, a novel system that integrates large language models with semantic alignment to the ATT&CK framework for CTI knowledge graph construction and threat reasoning. First, CTI-Thinker leverages in-context learning and LoRA-based fine-tuning to extract structured threat entities and relations. Then, it adopts vector-based alignment strategies to unify heterogeneous expressions, enabling entity normalization and knowledge fusion for constructing a high-quality CTI knowledge graph. Finally, a GraphRAG-based reasoning engine is built by incorporating the structured knowledge graph and external ATT&CK resources into a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) framework, enabling tactical-level inference and CTI-driven question answering. Experimental results demonstrate that CTI-Thinker accurately extracts threat entities and relations and constructs a reliable CTI knowledge graph. It also effectively infers attack intent and supports intelligent reasoning. The system outperforms state-of-the-art methods in precision, robustness, and generalizability, offering a scalable and semantically enriched solution for cyber threat analysis and defense.

Introduction
Recently, advanced persistent threats (APTs) (Yang et al. 2025), zero-day vulnerability exploitation (Rahimi et al. 2025), DDoS attacks (Tang et al. 2025), and PowerShell fileless attacks (Yang et al. 2023) have posed critical risks to national security, industrial production, and social stability. It is urgent to detect adversarial behaviors and implement proactive defense by leveraging threat intelligence knowledge. Cyber threat intelligence (CTI) refers to evidence-based knowledge of threats that documents critical information about cyber incidents or malicious activities, with the objective of supporting defense strategy formulation and adversarial behavior modeling (Ainslie et al. 2023; Hu et al. 2024). By extracting key entities (e.g., APT groups, attack tools, and vulnerability) along with their semantic relations from unstructured texts (e.g., security reports, alert logs, and threat bulletins), fragmented threat knowledge can be transformed into structured attack profiles and knowledge graphs, thereby enhancing the capacity of security systems to comprehend and reason over complex attack chains (Gao et al. 2021; Jo et al. 2022). However, existing open-source CTI resources commonly suffer from issues such as unstandardized structure and fragmented knowledge. Also, they often rely excessively on expert annotations and lack deep semantic modeling of advanced adversarial tactics and techniques (Ainslie et al. 2023). These limitations significantly constrain their effectiveness in tasks such as attack attribution, intent inference, and intelligent question answering. Furthermore, many current approaches fail to fully exploit CTI resources, neglecting the automated extraction of tactics, techniques, and procedures (TTPs) and indicators of compromise (IOCs), which in turn undermines the robustness and intelligence of threat detection and attribution systems (Zhao et al. 2024). Therefore, it is imperative to develop an intelligent system capable of automatic knowledge extraction and complex attack reasoning to enable the efficient transformation of unstructured CTI texts into structured security knowledge, thereby supporting advanced semantic inference. In the early stages, researchers primarily employed deep learning models (e.g., BiLSTM-CRF, BERT, and RoBERTa) and natural language processing (NLP) techniques to extract CTI entities and relations (Ranade et al. 2021; Yang et al. 2022). Several representative systems further integrated ontology-based multilingual entity extraction and knowledge graph updating, including CyberEyes (Fang et al. 2021), CSKG4APT (Ren et al. 2022), and SecureBERT (Aghaei et al. 2022). While these methods have shown promise in extracting threat-related information, their heavy reliance on manually annotated data limits adaptability to large-scale and multimodal
CTI corpora. Moreover, the separate modeling of entities and relations often fails to capture global semantic dependencies, resulting in error propagation and fragmented understanding. Also, the insufficient semantic alignment with TTPs restricts their effectiveness in supporting behavioral reasoning and high-level threat analysis. With the rapid advancement of large language models, their superior performance in NLP tasks has significantly expanded the capabilities of security text processing. Equipped with strong capabilities in language understanding and contextual modeling, LLMs are able to perform complex semantic parsing even under zero-shot and few-shot scenarios, thereby significantly reducing reliance on annotated data and gradually facilitating the evolution of CTI from manual analysis to semantic understanding (Huang et al. 2025; Chang et al. 2024). Recent studies have begun integrating LLMs into CTI extraction tasks. For instance, CTINEXUS (Cheng et al. 2025) leverages in-context learning strategies to construct knowledge graphs under few-shot settings. LLM-TIKG (Hu et al. 2024) employs LoRA-based finetuning and triple generation to accomplish entity and TTP extraction. GPT-RE (Wan et al. 2023) reformulates relation extraction as a question-answering process and incorporates semantic reasoning to improve the structured output. These approaches introduce novel paradigms for CTI extraction and threat analysis, thereby accelerating the development of structured threat intelligence systems. However, existing LLM-based threat intelligence extraction methods still face several challenges. First, some approaches focus on isolated entity or relation extraction tasks and lack unified modeling for both knowledge extraction and attack chain reasoning, which limits the construction of semantically complete CTI knowledge graphs. Second, real-world CTI corpora often contain a high degree of polysemy and ambiguity, and the absence of effective entity alignment and knowledge fusion techniques leads to semantic inconsistencies in the extracted knowledge. Third, some methods are highly sensitive to prompt formulation, prone to generating hallucinations, and exhibit limited capacity in attack chain reconstruction and deep reasoning due to the lack of graph-based structural assistance and insufficient handling of cross-paragraph dependencies and semantic inference. To address the aforementioned challenges, we propose CTI-Thinker, a threat knowledge graph construction and reasoning system enhanced by large language models and ATT&CK semantic mapping. Centered around an LLM architecture, the system integrates in-context learning (ICL), LoRA-based fine-tuning, and a GraphRAG-based

Background and related work
This section provides a concise overview of recent works in extracting cyber threat intelligence (CTI).
Threat entity recognition and relation extraction
A knowledge graph (KG) is a multi-relational graph structure where entities are represented as nodes and semantic relationships as edges. It is designed to model real-world concepts and their interrelations, enabling the organization, storage, and reasoning over complex and heterogeneous knowledge (Hogan et al. 2021). As a key approach for the structured representation of complex information, KGs have been widely applied in domains including search engines (Xiong et al. 2017), healthcare (Piya and Beheshti 2025), industrial control system (Li et al. 2024a), and social network analysis (Abu-Salih et al. 2021). These advances provide a solid foundation for their integration into cybersecurity (Ainslie et al. 2023). Threat entity recognition aims to identify key information entities (e.g., APT groups, malware, attack techniques, or vulnerabilities) from unstructured cybersecurity texts. Relation extraction captures semantic associations (e.g., use, attack) among these entities, constructing the relational structures of CTI knowledge graphs (Ainslie et al. 2023). Figure 1 illustrates how structured triples can be extracted from free-text descriptions. For example, Patchwork using PowerShell and exploiting CVE-201711882 can be represented as a knowledge graph to support downstream tasks (e.g., semantic reasoning or attack detection).
Traditional DL-based CTI Extraction. Previous research on CTI extraction methods has primarily relied on natural language processing and deep learning (DL) techniques, including conditional random field (CRF), convolutional neural networks (CNN), bidirectional long short-term memory network (BiLSTM), and Transformer-based architectures (Zhao et al. 2024). These approaches focused on classifying cybersecurity entities and their semantic relations. CyberEyes (Fang et al. 2021) is a GCN-based cybersecurity entity recognition system that models non-local dependencies through graph representations of inter-entity relationships. CyberRel (Guo et al. 2021) reformulated entity recognition and relation extraction as a unified multi-sequence labeling task. CSKG4APT (Ren et al. 2022) leveraged a multilingual BERTbased entity recognition model to support automatic graph updates, APT profiling, and attribution analysis. Yang et al. (2022) proposed an APT entity recognition model based on a BERT-BiLSTM-CRF architecture and constructed structured profiles to represent APT groups. In addition, Mouiche and Saad (2025) conducted a comprehensive review of threat entity recognition and relation modeling methods, offering a systematic reference framework for subsequent research.
Bert-based Variants for CTI Extraction. Since the introduction of Bert, its bidirectional semantic modeling has significantly enhanced the extraction of complex language patterns, outperforming LSTM and CNN models in handling long-range dependencies. Subsequently, various Bert-based pre-trained variants have been developed to further improve the performance of CTI extraction. Chen et al. (2021) proposed the Joint Bert model that integrated Bert embeddings with LSTM, ID-CNN, and CRF architectures to improve the recognition of software-related entities. CyBERT (Ranade et al. 2021), a domain-adapted model pre-trained on cybersecurityspecific corpora, enhanced contextual representation capabilities and enabled effective entity recognition in cybersecurity texts. Similarly, Aghaei et al. (2022) introduced SecureBERT that can recognize security entities and perform threat text classification tasks.
TTP-based Threat Behavior Extraction. However, traditional approaches often overlook the logic of the attack chain in cyber intrusions, making it difficult to effectively capture the tactics, techniques, and procedures (TTPs) of adversaries. To address this limitation, several knowledge extraction methods have been proposed to model attack processes and behaviors. Chen et al. (2025a) introduced EMGE that enhanced crosssentence relation extraction through semantic enrichment and connection modeling. TTPDrill (Husari et al. 2017) combined keyword-based retrieval and semantic
matching to extract TTPs and map them to ATT&CK.

Moreover, traditional approaches perform poorly in aligning adversarial TTPs and fail to infer attack behaviors. To address these challenges, LLM-based approaches for threat knowledge extraction have gradually emerged.
Large language models and retrieval-augmented generation techniques
Large Language Models. With the rapid advancement of deep learning, large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated strong capabilities in language modeling, semantic understanding, and contextual reasoning. Representative models include the BERT family (e.g., BERT, RoBERTa (Liu et al. 2019)), the GPT series (e.g., GPT-3, GPT-4 (Achiam et al. 2023)), and open-source foundation models (e.g., LLaMA (Touvron et al. 2023) and DeepSeek (Guo et al. 2025a)). In the cybersecurity domain, LLMs have shown great promise in extracting and reasoning over cyber threat intelligence. They can effectively identify entities, events, and relations from unstructured text, supporting the construction of extensible CTI knowledge graphs. Furthermore, their zero-shot and few-shot learning capabilities reduce the dependence on annotated data, advancing automated and intelligent threat analysis (Shi et al. 2024). However, current LLMs face several challenges in practical deployment. First, lacking external knowledge retrieval, they are prone to hallucinations that deviate from factual information (Ji et al. 2023; Huang et al. 2025). Second, their generation often lacks controllability, interpretability, and stability, particularly in cybersecurity
contexts with high contextual interference (Shi et al. 2023). Moreover, their understanding of threat semantics remains shallow, relying on pattern matching and struggling with polysemy, ambiguity, and subtle semantic variations, which limits deep reasoning and attack chain modeling (Srikanth et al. 2024).
Retrieval-Augmented Generation. To address the above limitations, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) has emerged as a promising solution (Lewis et al. 2020). RAG introduces an external retrieval module that semantically fetches relevant information from knowledge bases and combines it with the input for generation. This enhances factual accuracy, contextual completeness, and output controllability. RAG has shown strong performance in knowledge-intensive tasks and is increasingly applied to cybersecurity text processing (Rajapaksha et al. 2024). Recently, Microsoft proposed GraphRAG (Edge et al. 2024), which integrates graph structures and community-based partitioning to model long-range cross-document dependencies. This advancement supports APT chain reasoning, TTP extraction, and crosssource threat attribution. In summary, LLMs offer strong language understanding and generation capabilities, enabling structured threat intelligence representation and cybersecurity knowledge graph construction. The RAG framework further enhances factual consistency and contextual coherence. Accordingly, we propose a CTI entity and relation extraction approach that integrates LLM and RAG, along with a knowledge graph reasoning engine to support APT attack chain inference.
LLM-based threat intelligence extraction
General-domain Knowledge Extraction with LLMs. LLMs were initially applied to generaldomain knowledge extraction, demonstrating strong performance in automated KG construction on benchmark datasets. Representative works include SAC-KG (Chen et al. 2024a), which employed structureaware prompts for triple generation; LKD-KGC (Sun et al. 2025), an unsupervised framework leveraging interdocument semantics; and iText2KG (Lairgi et al. 2024), which adopted multi-round extraction and incremental graph building. To address data scarcity, Liu et al. (2024) proposed a zero-shot self-prompting strategy for relation extraction, while ACKG-LLM (Wang et al. 2025) ensured scalability through generative capabilities without retraining. These methods have achieved notable advances in entity recognition and triple generation, supporting high-quality KG construction. However, their reliance on open-domain corpora and standardized datasets limits applicability to complex vertical domains such as cybersecurity, which features domain-specific

LLM-Based CTI Extraction. In the early stages, researchers primarily used unfine-tuned large language models for CTI extraction. Fine-Tuned LLM-Based CTI Extraction. To overcome existing limitations, recent studies have explored fine-tuning strategies and prompt optimization to enhance the accuracy and robustness of CTI extraction (Chen et al. 2024b). AECR (Chen et al. 2025b) employed a fine-tuned LLM to extract attack techniques and integrated automatic verification mechanism to reduce hallucinations. LLM-TIKG (Hu et al. 2024) combined LoRA fine-tuning with large-scale models to build knowledge graphs from unstructured CTI and extract TTPs, effectively linking IoCs to high-level attack patterns. CTIBench (Alam et al. 2024) provided a benchmark for evaluating LLMs in CTI applications. LLMCloudHunter (Schwartz et al. 2025) generated candidate detection rules from textual and visual OSCTI data. In addition, LLM-based correlation techniques have been applied to attack detection (Du et al. 2024), autonomous cyber defense (Loevenich et al. 2025), incident QA (Sufi 2024), and phishing detection (Li et al. 2024b). However, these methods still struggle with deep semantic understanding and long-document extraction, often resulting in semantic drift, structural fragmentation, and redundant expressions.
CTI Extraction Using In-Context Learning in LLMs. Existing CTI extraction methods often struggle
to identify attack-level information (i.e., TTPs) due to limited contextual semantic awareness and poor handling of long-form text. The average CTI report contains about 408 words, frequently exceeding the context window of most LLMs (Xu et al. 2024). To overcome these challenges, enhanced approaches combining In-Context Learning (ICL) with semantic augmentation have been proposed. Representative methods include IntelEX, c-ICL, AttacKG+, and CTINEXUS. IntelEX (Xu et al. 2024) leveraged ICL and an LLM-based judgment module to extract TTPs from unstructured text. AttacKG+ (Zhang et al. 2025) used automated LLMdriven modeling to construct attack graphs, addressing semantic redundancy and structural fragmentation. The c-ICL framework (Mo et al. 2024) incorporated structureaware prompts and contrastive learning to improve triple extraction quality. CTINEXUS (Cheng et al. 2025) optimized ICL for few-shot cybersecurity knowledge graph construction, supporting long-distance relation prediction in complex CTI reports. LLM-based methods have advanced TTP extraction and improved semantic structure. However, several critical challenges still persist. These include weak cross-paragraph reasoning, hallucinations, and high sensitivity to prompt design, which reduce robustness and reasoning accuracy. To address these issues, recent research has explored threat reasoning approaches based on Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) and graph-enhanced techniques.
Enhanced Reasoning with RAG and Graph-based Techniques. The RAG paradigm has significantly improved retrieval accuracy and response generation in threat intelligence extraction. IntelEX (Xu et al. 2024) adopted GraphRAG to generate attack steps for red team simulation. APTSniffer (Xu et al. 2025), an encrypted APT traffic detection model, combined LLMs with RAG to transform raw traffic into interpretable reasoning traces. IntellBot (Arikkat et al. 2024), a security chatbot based on LLMs and RAG, supported threat intelligence and incident response. MalGTA (Guo et al. 2025b) combined dynamic sandbox with RAG to analyze malware tactics aligned with the ATT&CK framework, improving analytical accuracy and interpretability. Rajapaksha et al. (2024) applied a RAG-based QA model to assist in attack investigation and attribution. Jeon et al. (2024) compared Label Property Graph (LPG) and RDF graph models for RAG-based threat tracing. DroidTTP (Arikkat et al. 2025) employed RAG and LLM fine-tuning to map Android malware behaviors with ATT&CK. Fayyazi et al. (2024) contrasted supervised fine-tuning of encoder-only LLMs with RAG-based approaches for decoder-only LLMs in threat analysis.

Summary and research motivation
LLM-based threat intelligence extraction has made significant progress, particularly through the integration of ICL and RAG. However, most existing methods remain confined to isolated tasks (e.g., entity recognition or relation extraction) and lack a unified framework for threat knowledge modeling and reasoning. Challenges such as fragmented entity alignment, limited semantic integration, and the absence of modules for knowledge fusion, disambiguation, and attack chain reconstruction persist.
Furthermore, their reliance on lengthy prompt interactions leads to high token consumption and poor scalability. To overcome these limitations, we propose CTI-Thinker, a system that integrates semantic enhancement with advanced inference capabilities. CTI-Thinker unifies entity recognition, entity alignment, relation extraction, and ATT&CK-based semantic reasoning. Model design and implementation
Section 3 introduces the overall framework of CTIThinker, followed by a detailed overview of the implementation processes for each stage and its corresponding algorithms.
Overview
To enhance the extraction of cyber threat intelligence and the reasoning of attack behaviors, we innovatively design and implement CTI-Thinker. CTI-Thinker is an LLM-driven system for CTI extraction and reasoning that incorporates ATT&CK-based semantic mapping. It leverages in-context learning and fine-tuned LLMs to automate knowledge extraction, employs knowledge alignment to integrate threat profiles, and utilizes GraphRAG to enable question answering and attack scenario reasoning. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the overall architecture of CTI-Thinker comprises four key modules, which are described in detail below. Data Preprocessing. This stage aggregates multisource CTI data, including unstructured CTI reports, APT analysis documents, and vulnerability alerts. Prompt templates are constructed using LLMs to guide the generation of structured outputs (e.g., entities, relational triples, or QA pairs). The generated results are manually reviewed and refined based on expert knowledge. This process yields high-quality, instruction-tuned annotated
CTI corpora, thereby providing standardized inputs for downstream information extraction tasks.
Entity and Relation Extraction. To enhance the recognition of key entities and semantic relations within CTI-related corpora, large language models (e.g., LLaMA3-8b and DeepSeek) are fine-tuned using LowRank Adaptation (LoRA) techniques. In addition, an in-context learning (ICL) strategy is adopted, where a K-nearest neighbor (KNN) retriever embeds current and candidate reports into a high-dimensional space and selects the top-k most similar samples to construct customized prompt templates. Then, the fine-tuned model outputs structured relational triples in the form of 〈entity, relation, entity〉, covering key elements such as APT groups, attack methods, and targeted victims. These outputs serve as foundational semantic units for subsequent knowledge integration and reasoning.
Knowledge Alignment and Aggregation. This stage takes entity–relation triples as input and applies language model and knowledge chunking for representation learning. Using LoRA-tuned models and ICL, CTI entities and relations are coarsely grouped into semantically similar clusters. Vector similarity is used to align differing expressions of the same concepts. CTI-Thinker resolves ambiguity and redundancy by consolidating various mentions that refer to the same cybersecurity entities or relationships, thereby enabling effective threat knowledge integration (as illustrated in stage (3) of Fig. 2). The system subsequently auto-generates a structured CTI InfoBox encompassing key information (e.g., threat actors, geographic scope, attack events, tools, and vulnerabilities). Such output serves as the foundation for constructing a high-quality CTI knowledge graph, providing semantic support for threat reasoning and query tasks.
RAG-based Attack Inference. A reasoning framework based on Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is constructed to support automated cyber threat inference. Upon receiving a user query, the retriever module retrieves relevant external knowledge and graph-based information, which are subsequently used by the GraphRAG model to generate high-quality reasoning prompts. The fine-tuned LLM integrates structured knowledge graph content with contextual cues to produce CTI inference results compliant with STIX 2.1 and ATT&CK standards. The resulting system functions as an intelligent question-answering platform for automated APT threat analysis, enabling accurate reasoning over tactics, techniques, and procedures (TTPs), and mapping them to corresponding ATT&CK phases. CTI-Thinker enhances situational awareness and supports strategic decision-making in cybersecurity defense operations.

This paper proposes a novel two-stage knowledge alignment strategy that combines a fine-tuned LLM with vector-based similarity computation to achieve highquality normalization of CTI. This framework enables the effective unification of heterogeneous entities and relations from multiple sources, thereby facilitating the construction of consistent and semantically coherent threat knowledge graphs. To evaluate the performance of the proposed method, we conduct comparative experiments against three representative models: ECBERT, CTI-BERT, and ATTACK-BERT. As shown in Table 8, our method achieves an F1-score of 0.7349 for triple alignment and 0.8288 for entity alignment, outperforming all comparison models. Specifically, the proposed approach achieves an average improvement of approximately 5.81% in triple alignment and 5.50% in entity alignment over the three baseline methods. These results demonstrate its superior performance in integrating heterogeneous CTI knowledge. Overall, these findings confirm the advantages of our hierarchical alignment framework in promoting semantic consistency and cross-text integration. By leveraging the representational strength of SecureBERT and embedding-based similarity computation, the method significantly enhances the robustness and utility of CTI alignment, laying a solid foundation for constructing high-quality CTI knowledge graphs. Table 9 presents the performance of entity and relation alignment tasks at different similarity thresholds, ranging from 0.80 to 0.95. The results indicate that a threshold of 0.80 provides the best overall performance, achieving the highest F1-scores for both triple alignment (0.7718) and entity alignment (0.8383). At this threshold, the model captures a wider range of valid alignments, improving recall without sacrificing precision. We tested lower thresholds and found that 0.80 provides the best performance. Although thresholds below 0.80 showed a slight improvement in recall, further inspection revealed that entity alignment at these lower thresholds had little effect and was prone to overfitting the dataset. This overfitting leads to capturing unnecessary matches, which harms the model’s ability to generalize. Therefore, we concluded that further lowering the threshold does not provide meaningful improvements.

Discussion
In this section, we discuss some limitations of the current work as well as the future work.
Limitations
Limited generalization in complex attack contexts Although CTI-Thinker demonstrates strong performance in unstructured threat knowledge extraction, it still faces limitations in generalizing to complex attack contexts characterized by redundant expressions, heterogeneous data sources, and semantic ambiguity in APT reports. The model is prone to semantic drift or the generation of redundant triples, particularly in the absence of a clearly defined attack chain. These issues may lead to incorrect entity aggregation or omission, thereby affecting the completeness of the knowledge graph structure and the accuracy of downstream reasoning.
Challenges in multi-turn reasoning and interpretability While CTI-Thinker integrates a GraphRAG framework to support attack chain reasoning, its ability to maintain coherent context in multi-turn reasoning scenarios (e.g., causal tracing or tactical transitions) remains unstable. The interpretability and consistency of reasoning paths require further improvement for deployment. Moreover, enhanced integration with real-world threat artifacts (e.g., malware samples and event logs) is necessary to support deeper event-level reasoning and attack behavior attribution.
Future work
Cross-modal threat knowledge extraction. We will further evaluate the applicability and robustness of CTIThinker across large-scale, multi-source, and multilingual CTI corpora, assessing its effectiveness in knowledge extraction and reasoning across diverse languages and security incident categories. Additionally, we plan to explore prompt engineering strategies and knowledge modeling mechanisms that integrate multimodal inputs (e.g., malware and log data), aiming to extend CTIThinker’s capabilities to cross-modal threat intelligence extraction.
Enhancing attack chain interpretability via provenance graph modeling. Subsequent efforts will incorporate provenance graphs and the temporal dependencies of attack chain events to strengthen the logical relationships and sequential constraints among adversarial behaviors. This integration is expected to enhance the semantic coherence and interpretability of reasoning paths, thereby supporting more in-depth causal analysis and attribution of attack activities.
Conclusion
In this paper, we present CTI-Thinker, a novel system that integrates large language models (LLMs) with semantic alignment to the ATT&CK framework for constructing CTI knowledge graphs and enabling threat reasoning. CTI-Thinker leverages in-context learning (ICL) and LoRA-fine-tuned LLMs to extract threat entities and relations from unstructured CTI texts. It further employs a two-stage alignment mechanism combining semantic grouping and vector-based similarity to integrate heterogeneous CTI knowledge into a coherent graph. A GraphRAG-based reasoning engine is also introduced, supporting tactic–technique inference and threat-oriented question answering. Experimental results show that CTI-Thinker outperforms existing methods in both knowledge extraction and alignment tasks, achieving F1-scores of 0.8385 for entity recognition and 0.7689 for relation extraction, surpassing deep learning baselines, SOTA systems (e.g., ATTCKG+, LLM-TIKG), open-source large models (e.g., ChatGPT-4o, DeepSeekR1), and fine-tuned models (e.g., LoRA-LLama3-8B, LoRA-QWen, LoRA-Mistral-7B). In the alignment task, it achieves an average improvement of 5.50% in entity alignment and 5.81% in triplet alignment over baseline models, verifying its effectiveness in integrating heterogeneous entities and multi-source CTI expressions. Notably, CTI-Thinker aligns aliases, abbreviations, and semantic variants of APT groups, significantly enhancing the consistency and standardization of the resulting knowledge graph. Appendix
This appendix introduces the scoring system used in this study to evaluate cyber threat intelligence reasoning models. To comprehensively assess the models’ performance in attack reasoning tasks, we designed three scoring dimensions: accuracy, completeness, and relevance. These are explained as follows:
• Accuracy. This metric evaluates whether the information provided in the answer is accurate and consistent with established facts, data, or background knowledge. • Completeness. This metric assesses whether the answer comprehensively covers all relevant aspects of the question, ensuring that no key information is omitted. • Relevance. This metric measures whether the answer directly addresses the question and remains focused on its core content.
Additionally, we have defined detailed scoring criteria to clearly demonstrate how the answers are evaluated.
• Irrelevant or Incorrect (0 points). The answer is unrelated to the question or contains serious errors, making it unable to answer the question or completely incorrect.
• Partially Correct with Confusion (0.5 points). The answer contains some correct information but also includes inaccuracies or confusion.
• Completely Accurate and Detailed (1 point). The answer accurately and clearly addresses the question, lists relevant information in detail, and provides sufficient background explanation.
