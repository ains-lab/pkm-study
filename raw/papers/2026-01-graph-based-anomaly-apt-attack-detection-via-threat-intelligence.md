## Abstract

Among Advanced Persistent Threats in recent years, hackers have combined multiple defense evasion techniques to hide themselves from the detection of traditional antivirus software. For example, the combination of fileless malware and Living Off the Land techniques and abusing legitimate cloud services force the enterprises have gradually adopted the Endpoint Detection and Response (EDR) instead. However, EDR has the disadvantage that this tool may produce massive false alarms. This situation force security maintainer and analysts to be burdened with a large amount of additional analyses. We proposed an anomaly detection system based on graphs. First, we input a provenance graph containing threat intelligence constructed by the normal behaviors of the system. After that, the system learns the potential structured information from the provenance graph for detecting the abnormal behavior of a host. The results show that the proposed system can effectively detect abnormal event logs. Moreover, we reduce the number of false alarms by up to 97.67%. The improvement dramatically reduces the heavy burdens on the security maintainers from the analyses of the records. Furthermore, the performance of the designed system shows that the abnormal detection based on the graph neural network is superior to a traditional neural network.

I. INTRODUCTION
I
N RECENT years, advanced persistent threat (APT) attacks have been increasingly. APT attacks primarily target specific large-scale enterprises, governmental organizations, and national infrastructures. They involve highly complex technologies and even incorporate zero-day attacks on particular system services to achieve long-term and continuous lurking and infiltration of the target intranet. Hackers use APT attacks to gain access to major service hosts and steal sensitive data, such as technology blueprints, national policies, and personal user information. They also perform ransomware attacks by encrypting a large number of files on the target system and requesting for ransom from their targets. When an entity is subjected to such an attack, it may be forced to completely terminate its services, resulting in incalculable property losses and reputational damage. In 2018 and 2019, a hacker group called Chimera launched an attack against the high-technology semiconductor sector of Taiwan. They launched APT attacks against several enterprises and leaked crucial information, including chip design documents, software development packages, and source codes. These APT attacks lurked in the intranet of these enterprise for 416 days [1]. Recently, several enterprises have been the target of novel types of APT attacks. According to recent threat intelligence [2], hackers tend to abuse legal cloud services, such as Dropbox and Google Drive, as a command-and-control (C2) server or as a storage location for stolen confidential data. Compared with personal host connections, legal cloud service connections provide a high degree of confidentiality, which hackers utilize to confuse and circumvent systems that are capable of detecting IP or domain names. Currently, hackers tend to use “LivingOff-The-Land (LOTL)” attacks that do not have to install the code to launch a fileless malware. They execute malware in memory and use only built-in tools with legal signatures or whitelisted tools to invalidate traditional detection technologies and antivirus software based on attribute code matching and signature authentication. According to a recent analysis of 700,000 security incidents by Bitdefender in 2024 [3], the reliance on Living off the Land (LOTL) techniques has reached critical levels. The report reveals that 84% of high-severity attacks involved the use of legitimate system tools (LOLBins), such as netsh.exe and PowerShell, to evade detection and execute malicious operations. According to multiple APT attack investigation reports, in novel APT attacks, APT-based hacker groups tend to not only use the aforementioned escape strategy but also attack the supply chain of the target and attack the suppliers themselves to infect their software, source codes, or update mechanisms. This technique allows for using the suppliers as a medium of attack to indirectly attack the main target. This attack is called supply chain attack, and it aims to evade defense and detection systems that use sources provided by suppliers as a whitelist in order to easily infiltrate the main target [5], [6]. Traditional antivirus software and relevant technologies have difficulty defending against novel APT attacks, which combine multiple defensive and evasive techniques. However, according to several reports, data provenance offers a reliable APT attack detection strategy. Data provenance relies on converting the system behavior into a directed acyclic graph, which describes the information flow between the system’s main body (e.g.,
process) and target (e.g., files and network interfaces). In events with causal relationships in the provenance graphs, even if the time interval between the events is long, the effect is minimal. Therefore, even if the state of the system slightly differs before and after an APT attack, the abundant contextual information provided by the provenance graphs can be used to identify benevolent and malicious events. In 2013, MITRE released the Adversarial Tactics, Techniques, and Common Knowledge (ATT&CK) framework to address the diverse and complex APT attack techniques. MITRE ATT&CK is a common knowledge [7] base (for a detailed explanation, please consult the references Section II-A) integrated on the basis of the tactics, techniques, and procedures (TTPs) observed by experts specialized in information security in real-world scenarios. To enhance the performance of traditional antivirus software against novel APT attacks, several enterprises have started to adopt endpoint detection and response (EDR). Most of the available EDR tools are used to detect potential threats by matching the rules and system events defined by TTPs compiled in the MITRE ATT&CK knowledge base. Despite the aforementioned advantages, EDR tools have certain drawbacks, the most critical of which is the production of a large number of false alarms. For example, according to the T1053.005 scheduled task/job [8] technique, hackers may use system scheduling to complete execution and persistence tactics. However, in real-world scenarios, systems and managers may still use system scheduling to complete routine tasks automatically. Therefore, EDR tools are highly likely to generate a large number of false alarms, substantially increasing the analysis costs of information security maintenance personnel and analysts [9]. The purpose of this study is to address the inadequacies of traditional antivirus software and systems in detecting novel and complex APT attacks and to reduce the number of false alarms generated by matching based on TTP-defined rules. Accordingly, we propose a system capable of generating system events attached to TTP information and constructing provenance graphs with abundant contexts on the host, in accordance with open-source threat intelligence contributed by threat research units. To understand the normal behavior of a host in its normal state, this study employs graph representation learning for the extraction of graph structures from provenance graphs. Graph representation learning has been applied to various fields, including chemistry, recommender systems, and social networks. While graph representation learning encompasses several classical learning methods, one notable method is the graph neural networks (GNNs). This approach enables the extraction of deep structural features from graphs within large datasets, thereby facilitating their use in prediction and classification tasks. Provenance graphs serve as the input for the GNNs, which is then utilized to detect abnormal system behavior generated on the testing host. Although traditional anomaly detection methods such as Isolation Forest [10] and one-class support vector machines (SVMs) [11] have been widely applied in the field of cybersecurity, these static models often rely on manually extracted or fixeddimensional features. MITRE ATT&CK [7] is a common knowledge base compiled from attack strategies and techniques observed by information security experts in real-world scenarios. In total, 191 techniques and 386 seed techniques, along with their descriptions and instances, are summarized. Each technique has a single corresponding identification code (ID). For example, the ID of the OS Credential Dumping technique is T1003 [8], which describes how the attacker attempts to dump credentials from operation systems or software. In this scenario, the attacker typically uses hashing or plaintext methods to obtain account login information and credentials, which are then used to execute horizontal actions to access the host with high authority. According to empirical records, the hacker group APT28 [14] used Mimikatz [15] to execute an OS Credential Dumping operation and achieve credential access. The overall attack process is referred to as a TTP. ATT&CK compiles and organizes the TTPs of all known attackers, and these data are used as a common language between enterprises and information security industries. TTPs effectively exercise their universality in detection, analysis, threat information, attack simulation, evaluation, and engineering. For example, it can be used to trace hacker groups and their tactics and can also be used to examine the coverage or alleviation methods of enterprise information security defenders against known techniques.

III. THREAT MODEL AND SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE
This section describes the threat model and system framework of the proposed system.
A. Thread Model
In this study, a scenario in which the internal hosts of an enterprise are under APT attack was considered. The following hypotheses were proposed regarding threat models: Attackers are skilled in using defensive and evasive techniques, such as legal cloud services, fileless malware, and LOTL attacks, to hide their behavior among the numerous normal operations of the host. However, although long-term lurking is difficult to detect, malicious behavior remains distinct from normal behavior. Hardware Trojan and side-channel attacks are not considered. System monitoring tools record the system behavior of each host in the enterprise, and the matching rules correctly correspond to the ATT&CK technique. Complete event records generated from routine tasks (before an APT attack) as normal host behavior are collected. These event records are used as a training set for the proposed system model. APT attacks occur on each host in the enterprise with complete deployment of the proposed system for detection. Attackers cannot stop the operations of system monitoring tools or alter the event records. This implies that the event record integrity is consistently conserved. It is worth noting that the provenance record can be secured to prevent tampering. Previous works have proposed tamperevident logging mechanisms to ensure the integrity of event records. KennyLoggings [25] enforces synchronous integrity at the kernel layer, while Custos [26] provides a TEE-backed logging and auditing framework that detects log manipulation with minimal overhead. Therefore, the practical deployment of such tamper-evident systems offers an effective means to ensure the integrity of event records.

B. System Design
Figure 4 presents the framework of the proposed system. The event record generated in the host is used as the input. If an event record is marked with a matching ATT&CK technique, then it is called an alarm event. Subsequently, the system collects and analyzes the event records to construct provenance graphs and then stores these provenance graphs in a graph database. Data preprocessing and feature extraction are performed before the provenance graphs are input into the model. To facilitate the input of data into the model for calculation, raw data are embedded into a low-dimensional vector space. The process is then divided into two stages: a training stage and an execution stage. During the training stage, the provenance graphs constructed by the event records generated from the normal behavior of each host are used for training. During the execution stage, the trained model is used for abnormality detection. The detection results specify whether an event record is a threat (i.e., true alarm). To train individual modules for each host of same-type host clusters to learn the behavior generated by each host, the system is deployed in an actual enterprise network. Finally, the trained models corresponding to each host are used for abnormality detection. 1) Event Log Collction: Event record collectors and Sysmon [16] system monitoring tools are installed to each Windows host in the enterprise intranet. Sysmon monitors base system behavior and generates event records. The event record collectors then transmit the collected host event records to designated analysis engines (database) for storage. Sysmon configuration files are then generated by a modified version of Sysmon Modular [17]. The matching rules defined in these configuration files are proposed by a number of information security experts after threat analysis. Among these matching rules, 766 correspond to 104 ATT&CK techniques. As listed in the record examples of Table II], the matching rules provide a matching technique ID and a technique name for the event records generated by Sysmon. In response to tactics such as credential access, privilege escalation, and persistence, Sysmon Modular provides partial rules that match the techniques against these tactics. This process suggests that the event record types of Sysmon are visible against these highly threatening attack techniques.

All the received event records are then input into the model for training. Their features are then aggregated to obtain the new features of the source and target nodes and present the aggregated features. GraphSAGE as our learning architecture for several key reasons. GraphSAGE’s inductive neighborhood aggregation mechanism uses a selective graph traversal design to aggregate features and generate vectors for target nodes. This enables the model to generalize its learned representations to dynamically emerging, previously unseen processes, files, and network entities within the provenance graph, while also being capable of handling large-scale graphs and adapting to temporal evolution. The goal of using this model is to combine node features with structural context to learn a model of normal system behavior, which can then be used to identify anomalous system activities. In contrast, traditional supervised methods (such as SVMs or Isolation Forest) assume that individual samples are independent and thus cannot effectively handle the complex inter-node structure and relationships, making these approaches ill-suited for large-scale graphs and rapid generalization to unseen nodes. The downstream part is then connected to an MLP model, and a LogSoftmax function is used as a classifier to classify the ATT&CK technique associated with the rule_technique_id edge property. Execution stage: During the execution stage, the system uses the trained classifier to analyze each warning record. Here, a warning record refers to any event that Sysmon has flagged as an alarm. For each warning record, the classifier determines whether the event is truly malicious or is a benign event incorrectly flagged by Sysmon. The alarm outcomes are defined as follows: True Alarm: The warning record corresponds to a real malicious event. In this case, the classifier identifies that the alarmed event deviates from normal system behavior and reflects malicious activity. False Alarm: The warning record corresponds to a benign event incorrectly flagged by Sysmon. The classifier determines that the alarmed event is consistent with normal routine behavior. Through this process, the system distinguishes actual malicious events from Sysmon’s false alarms and achieves effective abnormality detection.

A. Implementation
In this study, Winlogbeat [27] was used as an event record collector to collect event records generated by Sysmon in the host. These event records were then delivered to a Kafka [28] message queue. Logstash [29] serves as a consumer to subscribe Kafka topics in order to obtain queue records. Here, Logstash continued to analyze, filter, and convert records into designated data formats. After processing, the data were transmitted to the Elasticsearch [30] search and analysis engine for storage. To access the event records stored in Elasticsearch, a PySpark [31] program was written. The data were then analyzed to obtain the nodes and edges required to construct complete provenance graphs. These provenance graphs were then stored in the Neo4j [32] graph database to be accessed and viewed by a graphical user interface. During the data preprocessing stage, the all-MiniLM-L6v2 [33] sentence transformer model provided by Sentence Transformers [34] was used to embed all raw node property data of the provenance graphs into the vector space. PyTorch Geometric [35] was then used to construct GNNs and traditional neural networks. Subsequently, the provenance graphs were input into the model for training, evaluation, and detection. The model is described in Section III-C4. A GraphSAGE GNN was used to embed the nodes, with parameter K set to 1 and a max pooling aggregate function selected. The number of hidden-layer neurons was 128. A configuration set was used for both the training and the execution stages.

The event records of a designated host were obtained from Elasticsearch. User behavior included the use of conferencing software, editing office documents, browsing websites, and other routine tasks. Event records generated on a computer on two separate days were first collected and then divided into two datasets: BENIGN and MALICIOUS. The BENIGN dataset (normal behavior) contained data pertaining to the execution of routine tasks, whereas the MALICIOUS dataset contained data pertaining to malicious user behavior with malicious files, associated with hacker attacks. G. Comparison
The results obtained in this study were not compared with those of other relevant studies because previous studies did not mark matching ATT&CK techniques of event records in public datasets. For example, the DARPA TC [40] dataset utilized by PROGRAPHER [41], FLASH [42] and ORTHRUS [43] is a product of the United States Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency. The data included in the dataset were collected from simulated hacker attacks by external professional red teams from internal hosts of enterprises. However, the dataset was not stored in the original Windows event record format; instead, it was stored after the analysis of event records by a self-defined data model. Therefore, accurately attaching matching ATT&CK techniques of events to the data was difficult. Consequently, the system was incapable of effectively utilizing the dataset to drive the proposed abnormality detection model. For this reason, Sysmon’s original alarm output naturally serves as the appropriate and meaningful baseline. The proposed method focuses on refining and reducing false alarms generated by Sysmon, rather than replacing Sysmon or benchmarking against general anomaly-detection models. Overall, previous studies relied on datasets preprocessed with MITRE’s custom data model, where events were manually analyzed and annotated—conditions that rarely occur in real-world environments. In contrast, our system directly processes raw Sysmon logs and aligns them with publicly available Sysmon Modular rules, enabling straightforward integration into EDR systems and offering clear advantages in practical deployment scenarios. Moreover, since Sysmon is widely deployed across enterprise Windows environments, the proposed approach naturally scales to large and diverse organizational settings without requiring additional customization or data preprocessing.
